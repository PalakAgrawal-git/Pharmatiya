01

Understanding of Pharmatiya

Pharmatiya Health is a specialist health economics, outcomes research and real-world evidence consultancy — a small senior team selling methodological depth, not headcount.

Read strictly from the Phase 1 brief and the current site, the company does four distinguishable things:

Evidence generation

Design and execution of HEOR and RWE studies: retrospective observational work, prospective pragmatic trials, epidemiology and burden-of-illness studies, cohort development. This includes protocol development, operationalisation, analysis and interpretation.

Analytics on real patient data

Work across payer, PBM, EMR and EHR sources, with stated experience in medical and pharmacy claims and external lab data. Methods named on the current About page include logistic regression, ANOVA, survival analysis, propensity scoring and machine learning on hierarchical files, executed in SAS and SPSS, with extraction via SQL over Hadoop, Teradata and TOAD.

Strategy and access advisory

Translating that evidence into commercial outcomes for pharma and device manufacturers: value propositions, market access strategy, value-based and outcomes-based contracts, care management programme design, and payer–provider proposals.

A software product

An AI-assisted evidence tool, referred to in the brief as NextGen AI and shipping today as RWE - Builder at app.pharmatiya.net. Its stated workflow is Feasibility → Retrospective → Pragmatic Outreach, with no PHI uploads and mandatory human expert review.

Naming to confirm

The brief calls the product NextGen AI; the live navigation calls it RWE - Builder. These appear to be the same product. Phase 1 needs a single decided public name, because the page URL, the navigation label and the CTA all depend on it. This document uses NextGen AI throughout, per the brief.

What the positioning is not

Nothing in the source material supports positioning Pharmatiya as a large CRO, a technology platform business, or a general management consultancy. The credible claim is a senior, methods-led evidence partner with 25+ years of operating history and direct payer-side experience — notably the build-out of an organic Outcomes Research division at Aetna, which is the single most concrete credential in the current copy and is presently buried in the middle of a wall of text.

03

UX problems

Ordered by commercial cost, not by how easy each is to fix.

  1. The site cannot capture a lead. Three pages invite the visitor to get in touch. None provides a means to do so. A buyer who wants to contact Pharmatiya must leave the site, and the one email address shown is not clickable. Verified: 0 form elements site-wide; footer email href is /contact, not mailto:
  2. The primary conversion action does not exist as a control. “Schedule your appointment” is set as a heading with body copy and no link. It reads as a call to action and behaves as decoration. Verified: no anchor or button within the block
  3. Services is unreachable from the navigation. The page describing what the company sells is omitted from the primary menu. Any visitor who lands on About or Contact from search has no path to it. Verified: primary nav contains Home, Products, About, Let’s Connect! only
  4. The three service teasers are undifferentiated and all lead to one page. A buyer cannot navigate to the specific service that matches their need, and the three descriptions are close enough in wording that they do not disambiguate themselves. Verified: all three anchors resolve to /services
  5. Placeholder images are shipped in production. Five images announce themselves as unfinished, on the two pages a first-time buyer is most likely to see. Verified: alt="Placeholder" ×4 on Home, ×1 on Contact
  6. Heading hierarchy is structurally broken. The homepage declares five separate H1 elements and then drops straight to H4, with no H2 or H3 anywhere. Screen-reader users get five competing page titles and no usable outline; search engines get no topical structure. Verified: H1 ×5 (“Research & Analytics”, “Our Services”, “Schedule your appointment”, “About our company”, “Contact Us”), then H4 ×5, no H2/H3
  7. An e-commerce cart is present on a consultancy site. A cart icon with a live counter sits in the header of every page and links to an empty checkout. It suggests the company sells products online, which it does not, and it is the clearest single signal that the site is an unedited template.
  8. The company’s real expertise is invisible above the fold and unreadable below it. All substantive proof — named datasets, statistical methods, the Aetna division build-out — sits on About in long unbroken paragraphs with no headings, no scannable structure and no visual entry points. The homepage, meanwhile, says nothing a competitor could not say.
  9. The differentiating product is a bare external link. RWE - Builder appears only as a navigation item pointing off-site. There is no explanation of what it does, how it works, what it does with patient data, or how to request access. A buyer cannot evaluate it without leaving.
  10. Navigation labels are template artefacts. “Products” resolves to /new-dropdown and contains one item that is not a purchasable product. “Let’s Connect!” is register-mismatched against a buyer audience of health economists and market access directors.
  11. The same contact block is duplicated across three pages. Repetition without variation trains visitors to ignore it, and it occupies the position where page-specific next steps should sit.
  12. No page states who Pharmatiya serves. Nowhere does the site name its buyer — pharma commercial, medical affairs, HEOR, payers, providers. A visitor must infer whether they are in the right place.

F. Visual problems

  • Recognisably a template. The layout, the cart, the folder-dropdown behaviour and the section rhythm are all Squarespace defaults, unmodified.
  • No visual hierarchy. Five H1s at similar sizes means nothing dominates; the eye has no entry point and no path.
  • Three identical cards. The service row is the exact pattern the brief rules out, and it is the only structural device on the homepage.
  • Imagery carries no information. Placeholder images occupy the positions where proof — a chart, a result, a person — should be doing work.
  • Typography is undifferentiated. One family, few weights, no editorial contrast between display, body, label and data. Nothing signals a research organisation.
  • Nothing looks like evidence. For a company whose product is statistical rigour, there is not a single chart, figure, table or number on the site.
  • Raster logo. Soft on retina displays; reads as low production value at exactly the moment credibility is being assessed.

Audiences by buying intent

Four intents, each with a different first question and a different evidence threshold. The architecture is built to answer them in the order they are asked.

1. The HEOR / RWE buyer

Typically: Director of HEOR, RWE lead, or evidence-generation manager inside a pharmaceutical or device company, scoping a study they cannot staff internally.

Trying to establish

  • Can this team design a defensible protocol?
  • Do they have access to the data my question needs?
  • Have they done this in my therapeutic area?
  • Will the output survive payer or journal scrutiny?

Objections

  • “Small shop — can they actually execute?”
  • “No published evidence of prior work.”
  • “Is this consultants or statisticians?”

Evidence needed before contact

  • Named methods, at the level of specificity a peer would use
  • Dataset types and access model
  • At least one worked case, even anonymised
  • Named deliverables — what actually arrives

Confidence signals

  • Correct vocabulary used unselfconsciously
  • Real statistical graphics, correctly drawn
  • Named credentials and payer-side history
  • Absence of marketing superlatives

Architectural implication: this buyer is why the Evidence page exists, and why methods and datasets get structured, scannable treatment rather than prose.

2. The commercial / market access decision-maker

Typically: brand, commercial or market access leadership. Less interested in method, more in whether the evidence will move a payer.

Needs quickly: what business problem this solves, what the engagement model is, what the output looks like in a payer conversation, and evidence of value-based contracting experience. Objection is commercial, not methodological — “will this change an access decision?” The Aetna history and the value-based contract experience are the assets here, which is why they are promoted out of the About page.

3. The referral visitor

Arrives with a name already recommended, often mid-conversation, frequently on a phone.

This visitor is verifying, not discovering, and gives the site seconds. They need to confirm within one screen that this is the right Pharmatiya, that it is a real firm with a real address, and that its scope matches what they were told. They then want a contact route immediately. Implication: the homepage must state what the company does and for whom above the fold, and a contact path must be reachable from every page without scrolling to the footer.

4. The NextGen AI prospect

Evaluating the tool, often sceptical of AI claims in a regulated context.

Their questions are almost entirely about trust: what the model touches, whether patient data leaves their control, who reviews output, and what happens when it is wrong. They need the workflow explained concretely, the human review step shown as part of the process rather than a disclaimer, and honest disclosure of the underlying model. Implication: trust content is designed as primary page structure on the NextGen AI page, not as fine print — and the CTA is a demo request, which is a lower commitment than a consultation.

Ordering principle

All four intents converge on one question: is this firm credible? Every architectural decision below prioritises answering that over describing services. This is why Evidence is a top-level page rather than a section of Services.

Required functionality by page

  • Home: static; no forms. Persistent header CTA.
  • Services: static; deep-linkable section anchors.
  • Evidence: static; optional gated-download UX (form → email delivery) if Pharmatiya supplies a sample.
  • NextGen AI: static; demo request form or routed link.
  • About: static; optimised team photography.
  • Contact: the only stateful page — segmented enquiry form with validation, Digest subscription, and an embedded or linked booking calendar if one is supplied.
Open dependency

Form handling requires a decision tied to input 10 (hosting). A static site needs an external endpoint — a form service, a serverless function, or the host’s built-in handler. This affects nothing in the architecture but must be settled before the Contact page is built in Week 4–5. Recommendation follows in the build phase.

↓

↓
Service block — problem → approach → method → deliverables
↓
Evidence — follows the “see the methods behind this” link to verify
↓
Contact — new-client route, referring service pre-noted
↓
Book a consultation

The longest and highest-value path. Its critical requirement is that the homepage teasers deep-link to individual services — the exact thing the current site fails to do.

Flow B — Proof-led evaluation

Home — proof figures catch attention before services do
↓
Evidence — enters at case studies
↓
Case study — challenge / method / dataset / approach / outcome
↓
Dataset coverage + therapeutic areas — checks own area is covered
↓
Contact — new-client route

The sceptical buyer’s path. They resist marketing and go looking for substantiation first, so proof figures on the homepage must link directly into Evidence rather than into Services.

Flow C — Product evaluation

Home — NextGen AI section, given real weight, not a footnote
↓
NextGen AI — what it is, in one screen
↓
Workflow — Feasibility → Retrospective → Pragmatic Outreach
↓
Trust section — no PHI uploads / mandatory human review / model disclosure
↓
Request a demo

Trust sits between understanding and action deliberately. A prospect who understands the workflow but not the data handling will not convert; placing trust after the workflow and before the CTA answers the objection at the moment it forms.

Flow D — Referral validation

Direct entry — often to Home or About, name already known
↓
About — track record, team, timeline: “is this a real firm?”
↓
Evidence — optional depth check
↓
Contact — frequently the header CTA, skipping the page body entirely

Short and impatient. Its requirement is a persistent header CTA so this visitor never has to hunt, and real team photography so the “real firm” question resolves visually in under a second.

Flow E — Mobile visitor

Home (mobile) — positioning + proof figures within first two screens
↓
Services (stacked) — scans service headings only, ignores body copy
↓
Relevant service — expands deliverables
↓
Sticky bottom CTA bar — persistent, thumb-reachable
↓
Contact — short form, route selected first

Mobile users scan headings and skip prose, so the mobile order front-loads proof and shortens every text block. The sticky bottom CTA exists because the mobile page is long and the header CTA scrolls away.

Copy direction for the hero

The headline must state the category and the buyer. Working draft, pending Pharmatiya review:

Health economics and real-world evidence for teams that have to defend the result.

Supporting line: “Twenty-five years designing HEOR and RWE studies across payer, provider and claims data — protocol through interpretation, for commercial, medical affairs and market access teams.”

Navigation behaviour

  • Sticky 56px header: wordmark and menu button only. The desktop CTA is dropped from the header to avoid crowding; the sticky bottom bar carries it instead.
  • Menu opens a full-screen overlay with six items at 20px and 56px touch targets. No folder drill-down — the Products folder is gone, so the current two-level “Folder: Products / Back” interaction disappears entirely.
  • Focus is trapped in the overlay while open; Escape closes; focus returns to the trigger. Background scroll is locked.
  • The overlay closes on route change; the current page is marked aria-current.

CTA behaviour

  • The sticky bottom bar appears once the hero scrolls out of view and hides while the closing CTA is on screen, so the same action never appears twice.
  • It reserves layout height rather than overlaying the footer, so the Digest field is never obscured.
  • All touch targets are at least 44×44px; primary buttons are 48px tall and full width.

Content stacking and simplification

  • Order changes: the hero figure moves below the copy and CTA, so the positioning statement and primary action are both visible without scrolling on a 320px screen.
  • Body copy is cut by roughly 40% in the service rows. Mobile visitors scan headings; the full text remains on the Services page.
  • Service graphics are dropped on mobile rather than shrunk. A forest plot at 375px is illegible, and shipping it as decoration costs bandwidth for no comprehension.
  • The Kaplan–Meier figure is simplified, not scaled: censoring marks and inline annotations are removed, stroke weight increases, only axis extremes are labelled.
  • The workflow diagram rotates from horizontal to vertical — the single adaptation that most affects comprehension of NextGen AI on a phone.
  • Coverage bars stack full-width with the label above each bar rather than beside it.

Typography and breakpoints

  • 320px: H1 at 28px. The tightest constraint is long medical terminology — “pharmacoepidemiology”, “cost-effectiveness”. Body text uses hyphens: auto and overflow-wrap: break-word to prevent horizontal overflow.
  • 375px: H1 at 32px, body 16px, line length ~38 characters.
  • 430px: H1 at 34px; 2×2 proof grid gains breathing room; no layout change.
  • Body text never drops below 16px, including captions on data figures (14px minimum).

Forms and data on mobile

  • Form fields are full width at 48px with labels above, never placeholder-as-label. Correct inputmode and autocomplete so the right keyboard appears.
  • Every data graphic has a text alternative; where a figure is dropped on mobile its key finding is retained as a caption line, so no information is mobile-only or desktop-only.
  • Any wide table scrolls inside its own container with a visible edge affordance — the page body never scrolls horizontally at any width.

Accessibility specifics for this page

Recommended visual direction

Scientific editorialism. The register of a clinical research publication, executed with the precision of a modern digital product — and no element of the current SaaS-template vocabulary.

The organising idea

Pharmatiya’s product is statistical rigour. The site should therefore be built out of the material that discipline actually produces: survival curves, forest plots, cohort diagrams, coverage matrices, number-at-risk tables. These are not illustrations placed near the content — they are the visual identity, occupying the positions a template would fill with stock photography.

This solves the brief’s hardest constraint honestly. A site cannot avoid looking AI-generated by adding decoration; it avoids it by having real content in the positions where decoration usually goes.

What we will not do

  • No stock photography of scientists or laboratories
  • No three identical feature cards
  • No gradient hero, no glow, no floating orbs
  • No glassmorphism
  • No decorative icon sets
  • No centred-everything layouts
  • No repeated identical section patterns
  • No abstract 3D or circuit-board AI imagery
  • No fake dashboards, statistics or testimonials
  • No large rounded cards or pill UI as a default

Layout rhythm

No two consecutive sections share a structure. The homepage sequence alternates deliberately: 60/40 hero → full-width figure row → 65/35 editorial → 35/65 inverted → 65/35 → full-bleed band → 40/60 dark panel → narrow offset column → asymmetric close. Rhythm comes from varying the split and the ground, not from adding ornament.

Cards appear in exactly three places, where grouping is genuinely informational: team members, contact route options, and trust guarantees. Everywhere else, content sits directly on the page separated by rules and space.

Grid

  • Desktop: max content width 1216px, 12 columns, 24px gutters, 48px outer margins. Full-bleed bands break the container deliberately and are the only elements that do.

  • Success: replaces the form with a confirmation stating what was sent, where it routed, and the expected response time.

Data components

  • Figure: the chart, a mono figure number (“Fig. 2.1”), a sentence-case caption stating the finding, and a source line. Captions state what the figure shows, never “chart showing data”.
  • Illustrative marker: any figure not drawn from real client data carries a persistent ILLUSTRATIVE DATA label in the caption area — a requirement of the brief and a condition of publishing.
  • Legend: inline labels placed at the end of each series wherever possible, rather than a detached key. Series are distinguished by both colour and dash pattern, so they survive greyscale and colour-blind viewing.
  • Annotation: mono, muted, with a hairline leader to the point it marks.
  • Table: hairline row rules, no vertical rules, no zebra striping, mono numerals with tabular-nums, sticky header on scroll, and an overflow-x container so the page never scrolls sideways.
  • Text alternative: every figure carries an accessible description of the finding, not of the shape. This is a WCAG AA requirement and it is also how the content reaches anyone reading on a phone with figures dropped.

Cards

Used in three places only — team members, contact routes, trust guarantees — each because the content is genuinely a set of parallel items. 1px border, 2px radius, no shadow, no hover lift. Everywhere else, rules and space do the grouping.

Accessibility baseline

  • WCAG 2.2 AA. Body text meets 4.5:1 minimum; large display text 3:1; UI borders and focus indicators 3:1.
  • One H1 per page, then a strictly descending outline — directly correcting the five-H1 homepage.
  • Full keyboard operability, a visible focus state on every interactive element, and a working skip link.
  • Landmark regions (header, nav, main, footer) with accessible names where repeated.
  • ARIA only where semantics fall short — the mobile menu, live-region validation, aria-current on navigation.
  • prefers-reduced-motion honoured; no motion is required to understand any content.
  • Meaningful alt text on every image. The word “Placeholder” will not appear in any alt attribute at launch.

Performance posture

Statically rendered, no client-side JavaScript except the mobile menu, the contact form’s conditional fields and validation. Charts are hand-authored inline SVG rather than a charting library — they are fixed figures, not live data, so a runtime dependency would cost hundreds of kilobytes for nothing. Photography served as AVIF/WebP with explicit dimensions to eliminate layout shift. Two font families, subset to Latin, font-display: swap, self-hosted and preloaded.
